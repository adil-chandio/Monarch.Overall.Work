# LONG-FORM SCRIPT V1 — "The Last Mammoths Recovered. Then They Vanished."

**Format:** English long-form documentary, master cut ≈14:00 / tight cut ≈12:30, 16:9, single English narrator (unchanged speed, no Short-style hypercutting).
**Structural device:** cold-case file — four expert "witnesses" (2017 genome, 2019 environment, human arrival, 2024 genome) each present a verdict, plus one missing witness (the final ~300 years).
**Status:** research-stage script draft for owner review. Nothing rendered, narrated, or published. No production-lock claim.

**Source anchors used in the script (all verified this session):**
- Rogers & Slatkin 2017, *PLoS Genetics* 13(3):e1006601, DOI 10.1371/journal.pgen.1006601 — two genomes; Oimyakon 45 ka; Wrangel 4,300 yr; Ne >43-fold lower; excess deletions / gene-affecting deletions / premature stop codons; olfactory-receptor and urinary-protein loss; **FOXQ1 two independent loss-of-function mutations → predicted satin coat**.
- Arppe et al. 2019, *Quaternary Science Reviews* 222:105884, DOI 10.1016/j.quascirev.2019.105884 — Wrangel collagen C/N unchanged to the end; Ukrainian plains ~15 ka; St Paul Island ~5.6 ka; diet shift to carbohydrate; bedrock weathering → possible drinking-water decline; rain-on-snow icing event hypothesis; humans "a few hundred years" after.
- Dehasque et al. 2024, *Cell*, DOI 10.1016/j.cell.2024.05.033 — 21 genomes (14 Wrangel, 7 pre-bottleneck); heterozygosity 0.75→0.43 per 1,000 bp (>40% loss); FROH 10.0%→41.6%; MHC diversity −49.2% vs −42% (genes) / −43.2% (genome-wide); deletion bases +36% (0.78→1.06 Mb); bottleneck Ne ≤8, recovery Ne 200–300 within ~20 generations; high-impact variants decreased (purging), moderate-impact increased (drift); coverage sensitivity of mutation-load comparison; youngest genome Wra4.3K = 4,333 cal BP; **dataset lacks the final ten generations**; earliest human on Wrangel ~3,600 cal BP.
- Guinet et al. 2025, *Cell*, DOI 10.1016/j.cell.2025.08.003 — 483 specimens, 440 newly sequenced, six host-associated microbial groups; 1.1 Ma steppe-mammoth *Erysipelothrix*; pathogen relatives present; **health impact uncertain**.

**Language rules applied:** effective population size is called a *genetic estimate, not a head count*; predicted effects are called *predicted*; calibrated BP is never converted into a calendar death year; every reconstruction visual is labelled an illustration; the microbe work is explicitly *not* the cause.

### COLD OPEN — 0:00–1:12
*[VISUAL: black → single adult mammoth in blue-hour snow, slow breath. Hair strands on the shoulder dissolve into a DNA sequence graphic. Hard cut to a black card.]*

**VO:**
"Here is a mammoth that lost its insulation. Not because it moved somewhere warm — because two mutations in its DNA switched off the gene that makes wool. That animal lived on Wrangel Island, in the Arctic Ocean, a few thousand years ago.

Now the strange part. The very same genome told the scientists who read it: this population was not dying out. It had *recovered*.

Both things are true. And that is exactly why four different teams of scientists, using four different tools, have each given you a different reason for how the last mammoths died — and not one of them has the final witness.

Here is what we're going to do. We take all four: the 2017 genome study, the 2019 climate-and-water study, the human arrival evidence, and the 2024 analysis of twenty-one genomes. We test what each one can actually support. And at the end, I'll tell you precisely what is proven, what is plausible, what is not in the file at all — and why the hole in this case is not a small detail."

### 1 · THE ISLAND NOBODY LEFT — 1:12–2:22

**VO:**
"Ten thousand years ago, sea levels rose and the Arctic coastline moved. A population of mammoths on the Siberian mainland found itself cut off on an island in the Arctic Ocean — Wrangel — with no way back and no way forward. They were trapped there. And for thousands of years, they were the last mammoths on Earth.

Which makes their ending the strangest in the whole record. Because other populations went first, and they went *differently*.

The Ukrainian-Russian plains lost their mammoths about fifteen thousand years ago. St Paul Island in Alaska lost its herd about five thousand six hundred years ago. And in both of those cases, the chemistry of the bones changed before the end. The diets shifted. The world around them shifted. They died inside a change.

Wrangel's mammoths did not do that. Their bone chemistry barely moved. To the isotope scientists of 2019, they vanished from the middle of stable, favourable conditions.

Stable. Good conditions. And then — gone."

*[VISUAL: map of the Arctic; mainland coastline rising; Wrangel highlighted; three timeline cards — Plains 15 ka, St Paul 5.6 ka, Wrangel ~4 ka. The Wrangel card keeps pulsing.]*
*[CASE BOARD: first card placed — "GENOME".]*

---

### 2 · WITNESS ONE: THE GENOME — 2:22–4:22

**VO:**
"So what killed the last of them? Let's watch what happens when four different teams get hold of the same evidence.

The first witness is DNA. In 2017, two researchers took the complete genomes of two woolly mammoths and put them side by side. One was from the Siberian mainland, forty-five thousand years old, back when mammoths were everywhere — a healthy, typical genome. The other was from Wrangel, dated to about four thousand three hundred years ago. The very end.

And they found the island animal carried an *excess* of damage. More deletions overall. More of those deletions landing directly inside working genes. More premature stop codons — genetic sentences that cut a gene off in the middle, so the gene never finishes its job. Its effective population size — a genetic estimate, not a head count — was more than forty-three times smaller than the mainland's.

Genomic meltdown. That was the reading. A small population, breeding among relatives, accumulating damage until the whole system broke. And for years, the woolly mammoth was the textbook example of that story.

But one detail from that study is so human it sticks with you. The same island genome carried **two independent losses of function in the gene called FOXQ1**. FOXQ1 is what gives an animal a smooth, satin-like coat instead of a shaggy one.

Read that again. A mammoth, losing its wool, on an Arctic island — by genetic accident. No predator, no climate. Just a shrinking gene pool with fewer and fewer different versions of that gene left to breed with, and a mutation that spreads because nothing else is competing with it.

In 2017, with only two genomes, this was the best answer available: the genes broke, and the herd followed.

Hold onto that. Because in 2024, somebody looked again — with twenty-one genomes."

*[VISUAL: two genome "cards" side by side; deletion marks accumulate on the right-hand card; FOXQ1 label pulses; a tiny silhouette of a smooth-coated mammoth next to a shaggy one, clearly marked ILLUSTRATION.]*
*[CASE BOARD: GENOME card gets a red string to "MELTDOWN?"]*

### 3 · WITNESS TWO: THE ENVIRONMENT — 4:22–6:12

**VO:**
"Second witness: the bones themselves. Not the DNA — the chemistry.

Strontium and sulfur tell you what the water and the rock were doing. Carbon and nitrogen tell you what the animals were eating. In 2019, a team from Helsinki, Tübingen and the Russian Academy of Sciences measured all four across forty thousand years of mammoth remains — Siberia, Alaska, the Yukon, Wrangel.

And they found the strange thing. As the world warmed, roughly ten thousand years ago, the plains and St Paul populations changed: their isotope values shifted, meaning their diets and their surroundings changed with them. But Wrangel's collagen carbon and nitrogen values stayed flat, right up to the end.

Not struggling. Not starving. Just — there.

Which means the chemistry was not pointing at a slow environmental squeeze. So the 2019 team asked a different question: what could kill a healthy-looking herd *fast*? And they landed on something almost Biblical. Rain falling on snow, freezing overnight into a solid sheet of ice across the whole island, sealing every meal underneath it. Not a long drought. Not a gradual decline. One season where the food was right there and the mammoths could not reach it.

They found a second, slower worry at the same time. Sulfur and strontium in the bones suggest the bedrock was weathering harder toward the end of the island's mammoth era — which can mean the drinking water was getting worse.

Put those together and you get a genuinely uncomfortable picture: a small, genetically strained herd, drinking poorer water, on an island with no exit route, hit by one bad season.

Second verdict. Genes plus weather plus water. And still, not one cause."

*[VISUAL: isotope bar chart that stays flat for Wrangel while others tilt; then a night scene of rain on snow hardening into a sheet; a water-clarity graphic that dulls at the end.]*
*[CASE BOARD: CLIMATE card placed, stringed to "ICING?" and "WATER".]*

### 4 · WITNESS THREE: US — 6:12–7:16

**VO:**
"Third witness: us.

Humans hunted mammoths for thousands of years. They were enormous, slow, and full of meat. So the obvious suspect for the last ones is obvious.

Here is the problem. The earliest archaeological evidence of people on Wrangel Island dates to roughly three thousand six hundred years before present. The last mammoth bones are *older* than that. Human presence shows up a few centuries after the mammoths are already gone.

That does not mean humans had nothing to do with it. The 2019 authors were careful: the chance of finding evidence of humans hunting mammoths on that island is very small, so a human contribution cannot be ruled out.

But look at what's happening. We are three witnesses in, and the honest answer keeps pointing at the same gap. The people arrived just after the story ended. Whatever finished this happened in the part nobody was there for."

*[VISUAL: footprint/trowel-plot graphic of an early human site on Wrangel; a timeline cursor slides past the last mammoth bone marker and stops in empty space.]*
*[CASE BOARD: HUMANS card placed, stringed to "TOO LATE?".]*

### 5 · WITNESS FOUR: THE BIGGER GENOME — 7:16–10:14

**VO:**
"Fourth witness: the same DNA, re-examined with far more of it.

In 2024, a team from Sweden's SciLifeLab and collaborators published twenty-one woolly mammoth genomes in *Cell*. Seven from before the isolation, fourteen from Wrangel afterwards, spanning fifty thousand years — including one of the most recent mammoth genomes ever sequenced.

The verdict was not what the textbook expected.

The island herd *did* take a brutal hit. Heterozygosity — the genetic variety a population carries — dropped by more than forty percent. Runs of homozygosity, the stretches of genome where an animal inherits an identical copy from both parents, roughly quadrupled: from about ten percent of the genome to over forty percent. And the major histocompatibility complex — the gene family that helps the immune system fight disease — lost more diversity than the genome as a whole. Yes. The bottleneck was real, and it was severe.

But then the finding that changes the story. The population *recovered*. The modelling says the effective population size — again, a genetic estimate rather than a head count — fell to around eight at the worst point, then rebounded to a few hundred within roughly twenty generations, and stayed there. There is no sign the survivors were breeding with close relatives. For thousands of years, the numbers were not collapsing.

Which leaves us with the uncomfortable question. If the population was recovering, what killed it?

And this is where the study gets genuinely clever — and where the story turns on a technicality almost nobody notices.

Genome-wide diversity and inbreeding are not the only things you can measure. You can also count predicted harmful mutations. To do that fairly, you have to account for how much of the genome you were able to read in each individual. Think of it as two windows into the same room. If one window is bigger, you might count more damage through it, even if the room is not worse.

The 2024 authors tested exactly that, corrected for coverage, and found something that contradicts the clean meltdown story. The *most damaging* predicted mutations went **down** — they were being purged, because in a small inbred population those harmful variants get exposed and weeded out by selection. Meanwhile, moderately harmful variants went **up**. Purifying selection was not strong enough to keep pace with genetic drift.

Which means the last herd was, in a strange way, cleaner of the worst defects than its healthy, plentiful ancestors on the mainland — while slowly accumulating a subtler kind of damage. And in the very last mammoth genome we have, dated to about four thousand three hundred years before present, there is no spike of inbreeding and no dramatic jump in mutation load. No accelerating collapse.

Recovered. And then gone."

*[VISUAL: real figure-style cards rebuilt as clean motion graphics — heterozygosity bar dropping, FROH rings filling, MHC bar lowest. Then the recovery curve rising. Then a labelled ILLUSTRATION of two windows onto the same room, one wide, one narrow, with unequal marks.]*
*[CASE BOARD: LARGER GENOME card placed; string to "RECOVERY".]*

### 6 · THE MISSING WITNESS — 10:14–11:52

**VO:**
"So — four witnesses. And every single one of them is pointing at the same missing piece.

Here it is. The 2024 study contains no genomes from the final ten generations of the island population: roughly the last three hundred years. The youngest genome we have stops a few centuries short of the end.

Everything we just watched happened *before* the ending. We know the setup. We know the herd was doing okay. We do not have the moment.

And here is the newest lead, because this case is still open. In 2025, a team in Stockholm reported the oldest host-associated bacterial DNA yet recovered from mammoth remains — hundreds of specimens, and among the bacteria were relatives of known pathogens.

Now be careful here, because this is exactly where videos like this one go wrong. Finding bacteria related to pathogens is *not* the same as finding the thing that killed the mammoths. Those sequences are degraded, they are comparisons, and the study itself says the health impact is uncertain. It also reaches back far past the island herd — including a mammoth from about a million years ago.

What it gives us is a new way to ask the question: stop reading only the animal's own genome, and read everything else in the bone. Authentication, contamination control, honest comparison.

But the diagnosis is not in the file. And anyone who tells you they have the killer for Wrangel is selling you a story, not a finding."

*[VISUAL: the case board with the last slot EMPTY and lit; then a micrograph-style plate of bacterial DNA; then the empty slot again.]*
*[ON-SCREEN: "THE LAST ~300 YEARS: NO DATA"]*

### 7 · THE VERDICT — 11:52–12:56

**VO:**
"So where does that leave us? Let's do this properly. Three columns.

**Supported by the evidence.** A severe bottleneck when rising seas cut the herd off. Real, measurable genetic damage: less diversity, more homozygosity, a weakened immune gene family. A demographic recovery after that bottleneck, sustained for thousands of years. Environmental signals of worsening drinking water. And a final stretch of roughly the last three hundred years for which we simply have no data.

**Plausible, not proven.** A sudden event — an icing season, a disease outbreak — hitting a population with reduced adaptive potential and worse water. A tipping point in the accumulated, subtler mutations. A small human contribution that left no archaeological trace.

**Not supported.** Any single named killer. Any claim that the genomes alone prove the cause. Any claim that the mammoths were simply too inbred to survive — the 2024 data specifically contradict the cleanest version of that story.

And notice: these columns do not fight each other. A small, isolated, genetically strained population, plus one bad season, plus a shrinking margin for error — that is not a contradiction. That is compounding.

Which is why the island matters. Wrangel was an experiment in what happens when a species gets cut off from everything that could help it: other herds, other genes, anywhere else to go. And it held for thousands of years. It did not fail slowly. It failed, and then it was over, and the last stretch of evidence burned with it.

We are left with a cold case. A small population. A stable-looking world. A genome that said things were improving. And three hundred years that nobody was there to watch."

*[VISUAL: three columns build in sequence, each item with its source tag. Then the case board: three cards connected by red string, one empty slot, camera slowly pushes in on the empty slot.]*

---

### 8 · ENDING — 12:56–14:26

**VO:**
"Which is the part worth keeping.

Every one of the four witnesses told a true story. The genome was telling you the population was damaged. The chemistry was telling you the island was stable, then the water worsened. The archaeologists were telling you people arrived just a little too late to matter. And the biggest genome study of all said the herd had actually come back.

The mistake would be to grab whichever one sounds most like a cause and call the case closed. The truth is more useful than that. For the last mammoths on Earth, no single thing has to be true to explain the ending. A shrinking gene pool, a worsening island, and one unlucky season are not three mysteries. They are one mystery with three faces.

And if that sounds like a story about mammoths, it is not really. There are populations alive today living out that exact experiment — cut off, small, watched closely, waiting for a season that does not have to be extreme to be final.

We do not get to watch most extinctions happen. The mammoths of Wrangel are the rare ones where the mystery is shaped like a hole in a file: three hundred years, no data, no witness.

So the last question is not what killed them. It is what we are still not looking at."

*[VISUAL: the empty slot dims; the board dissolves to the Arctic at dusk, one distant shape walking into fog. End on a source card, not a subscribe card.]*

## What this script deliberately does NOT do

- No invented killer, no "the virus that killed the mammoths," no fake "the DNA finally solved it" reveal.
- No doctored scientist quotes, no invented interview lines, no dramatised "final mammoth" monologue.
- No animated head-count of "8 mammoths became 300" presented as census fact; Ne is labelled as a genetic estimate.
- No calendar conversion of calibrated radiocarbon ages into a confident year of extinction.
- No claim that a competitor has or has not covered any of this — that comparison is not established.
- No promise of virality, CTR, or a specific view outcome; this is a script, not a forecast.
