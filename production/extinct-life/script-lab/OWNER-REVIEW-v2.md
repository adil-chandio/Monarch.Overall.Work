## B01 — The animal that arrived as dirt

### English

A mastodon turned up in northern Greenland. Not as a skeleton. As broken DNA in dirt roughly two million years old. That is the surprise reported in a study published in 2022: a trace of an elephant-like animal, in a place that is now a polar desert. But the animal is only half the puzzle. How did anything readable survive that long? And how do you know those tiny pieces really belong to the animal you think they do? The samples came from the Kap Copenhagen Formation. Plant remains had already hinted at an older, greener landscape. The DNA opened another part of that archive, especially the animals. To understand that discovery, follow what a life can leave behind—even when its body is gone.

## B02 — How a life becomes a trace

### English

A living thing does not have to become a fossil to enter the record. Cells and other biological material reach the surroundings as organisms grow, shed, decay and die. Water can carry that material into sediment. The result is environmental DNA: fragments from different lives, mixed into the same deposit. Imagine several field guides shredded into one pile. A surviving scrap might identify one animal. It cannot tell you which other animal stood beside it. This is not a sealed message from a single body. It is a mixed archive. That sounds like a weakness, until you consider what it allows: a trace of an animal without finding its bones. But first, one problem should have erased much of the evidence. DNA breaks down.

## B03 — The survival problem

### English

Break a sentence into smaller and smaller pieces, and eventually its meaning becomes hard to recover. Something similar happens as DNA fragments. The Greenland finding needed conditions that could help preserve some of those pieces. Long cold conditions are part of the explanation. So are minerals. DNA can bind to mineral surfaces, and that binding may help protect fragments against breakdown. The dirt is not merely the box holding the evidence. Its chemistry can influence what remains. There is a catch: that same binding can make surviving material harder to extract. Our close-up illustrates that mechanism; it is not a recording from inside the sample. And protection is not immortality. Many traces are lost. What researchers recover is the surviving fraction, not everything that once existed. Which creates the next challenge: a scrap can survive and still be difficult to identify.

## B04 — How to read—and be fooled

### English

Researchers extract genetic material, read its sequence and compare it with reference DNA. A useful match can suggest where a fragment belongs. But a very short stretch may fit several relatives. And the reference collection is uneven: some organisms are represented much better than others. So the question is not, Which exciting animal could this be? It is, How specific an identification can the evidence support? There is another trap. A newer contaminant can be mistaken for the ancient signal you wanted. That is why contamination and misleading matches must be tested, not edited out of the story. The comparison on screen is an illustration, not the study's actual reads. Researchers also examined characteristic damage in the ancient DNA. Damage is not just loss; it can help test whether a signal is genuinely old.

## B05 — The community, not just the giant

### English

Now the fragments begin to add up. The study reported evidence associated with hares, reindeer, geese, rodents and a mastodon lineage, alongside plants. The giant is memorable. But the more revealing result is the company around it. Poplar, birch, conifers, shrubs and herbs point towards a community very different from today's polar desert—not a simple copy of one familiar modern forest. This was not greenery invented entirely from DNA. Pollen and visible plant remains had already supplied clues. The genetic evidence added another route in, especially for animals with scarce fossil traces. Still, a lineage is not a portrait. These fragments do not give us the mastodon's exact colour, size or behaviour. We can recover part of the community without recovering every detail of its members.

## B06 — The scene you must not read literally

### English

Picture the giant passing a tree while a goose rises from the water. It feels like one moment. The sediment does not prove that moment happened. Water can move biological material from elsewhere. A layer can gather it over time. We have combined surviving clues into a scene, not recovered a film of an afternoon. There is an equally important mistake in the other direction. If an animal's DNA is missing, that does not prove the animal was absent. Perhaps its material did not survive, was not sampled, or could not be recognised well enough. The archive has filters. An honest reconstruction lets you see both its evidence and its gaps. That does not make the discovery smaller. It makes the difference between what we know and what we have drawn.

## B07 — Why the reconstruction holds

### English

That is why the DNA should not stand alone. Pollen, plant remains and geological evidence provide other ways to investigate the same landscape. The study found plant groups in DNA that were not recorded there as pollen or larger remains. Each method has its own blind spots. Even the age comes from investigating the deposits, not reading a birthday written on a molecule. Roughly two million years is the scale of this discovery; it is not an exact date for every fragment. And the result is not a complete inventory. Some organisms remain invisible. Yet the supported pieces still change the picture: a northern landscape held a combination of lives we would not expect from looking at it today. The gaps remain. So does the achievement. Neither needs to be hidden for the other to matter.

## B08 — What the ground kept

### English

So what came out of that dirt? Not a frozen mastodon waiting to wake up. Not a complete genome ready to rebuild one. Something less complete, but still extraordinary: fragments from which part of a vanished community became readable. Return to the place where we began. The forest on screen is our reconstruction. The genetic traces behind it are evidence. The ground does not have a memory; it has layers, chemistry and material that survived when much else did not. In these Greenland deposits, enough remained to show that an almost empty-looking landscape once supported a very different world. The discovery is not that the past came back intact. It is that the past did not disappear completely. The animal is gone. The story isn't. This is Extinct Life.
