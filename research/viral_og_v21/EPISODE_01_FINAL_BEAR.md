## 2. Narration script (~1,530 words → ~8:55 at 172 wpm)

> Delivery: low, calm, close. Har `[VERIFY]` record se pehle check karna hai (§6).

### COLD OPEN — 0:00–0:40

Thirty-two thousand years ago, in a cave in what is now southern France, somebody did something strange.
They walked deep into the dark, past the place where the light from the entrance gives up, and they put a bear's skull on a rock. Then they left it there.
Not thrown. Not dropped. **Placed.**
We don't know what they were thinking. We weren't there. But we know what the room looked like, because it's still there.
And that skull belonged to an animal that was bigger than any bear alive today — an animal that had been using that cave, and every cave like it, for a hundred thousand years before we ever came in.
This is the story of the largest bear that ever lived alongside us. And of what happened when two species decided they wanted the same house.
[VERIFY: Chauvet Cave bear skull placed on a rock — attributed to Chauvet-Pont-d'Arc; dated ~32,000 BP; confirm the exact context and that it is not a natural rock fall]

### CHAPTER 1 — The bear that could look a man in the eye — 0:40–2:10

Start with the size, because the size is the whole point.
A modern brown bear is already the biggest land predator most of us will ever see. A big male can stand well over two metres tall and weigh as much as four adult men.
The cave bear was bigger. Not a monster from a movie — but bigger in the way that matters when you're the one at the cave mouth. Heavier, longer, with a steeper forehead and a skull that makes a modern bear look ordinary.
[VERIFY: Ursus spelaeus mass estimates (commonly ~350–600 kg, some males larger) and the "larger than modern brown bear" claim — cite the specific morphometric study]

Here's the twist nobody expects: **the cave bear was mostly a vegetarian.**
The teeth say it. The isotopes say it. This was not an animal built to chase you across a plain. It was built to sit in a dark room and eat an enormous amount of plants, then sleep.
Which should make it harmless — and that's exactly the wrong conclusion.
Because "vegetarian" doesn't mean "safe". It means **it was not afraid of us.** A predator that hunts you has a reason to leave when you're not worth the risk. A bear that simply wants its bedroom back does not negotiate.

### CHAPTER 2 — We moved into their house — 2:10–3:40

When modern humans reached Europe, roughly forty-five thousand years ago, they found a continent that already had tenants.
Bears were everywhere. And not just in the wilderness — in the **caves**, which is where they gave birth, hibernated, and died. Thousands of bear skeletons have been pulled out of single cave systems. Not because bears were killed there. Because they went there to sleep, and some of them never woke up.
[VERIFY: cave bear bone accumulations in Drachenhöhle (Austria), Sloup (Czechia), and similar — counts and interpretation]

Now put a human family in that landscape. It's winter. You need shelter, and shelter in a limestone country means one thing: a cave.
The same cave. The same warm, dry, dark cave that a bear is currently sleeping in.
So you have a choice. Find another cave — and there might not be one within a day's walk. Or take this one.
For around twenty thousand years, humans and cave bears in Europe were competing for the same real estate. That is not a metaphor. It's the plain reading of the archaeology: the same caves, used by both, sometimes in the same layers.
[VERIFY: direct evidence of alternating/interleaved human and cave bear occupation at named sites]

### CHAPTER 3 — What a fight actually looks like in the record — 3:40–5:05

We'd love a scene. Two figures, a spear, a roar. We don't have that. We have bones.
And bones are honest.
Some cave bear bones carry cut marks — the thin, straight slices left by a stone tool. Some carry impact fractures from being broken open for marrow. That means humans were not only in these caves. They were eating bears.
[VERIFY: cut-marked and marrow-fractured cave bear bones — named sites and the specific studies]

The other direction is rarer and worse. Human bones with carnivore tooth marks, in caves, in layers where bears were also present.
Rare — but not zero. And "rare" is exactly what you'd expect if the encounter usually went one way.
[VERIFY: human remains with carnivore modification at Palaeolithic cave sites — state the actual scarcity honestly, do not inflate]

Here's the part that changes how you picture it. Killing a bear in a cave is not like hunting on open ground. You cannot surround it. You cannot see. The sound of your own footsteps comes back at you off the walls. There is one exit, and you are standing in it.
Whatever they did, they did it in the dark, with a fire, and with a very small amount of room for error.

### CHAPTER 4 — The skulls on the rocks — 5:05–6:35

Now back to the room we started in.
In more than one cave, researchers found cave bear skulls that seemed to have been **arranged**: set on ledges, placed in niches, sometimes with long bones crossed underneath.
The most famous argue about is Regourdou, in France, and Drachenhöhle in Austria. The claim that grew out of these finds was thrilling: a bear cult. Our first religion, and it was about an animal.
[VERIFY: Regourdou and Drachenhöhle bear skull/bone arrangements — original reports and their critiques]

Then the sceptics arrived, and they were right about a lot.
A cave is a terrible place to preserve a story. Bones roll. Water moves them. Later people move them. A skull on a ledge might be a skull that fell, or slid, or was pushed by ice.
So what's left, after you remove everything that could be an accident?
Something narrower, and honestly more interesting: humans and bears were using the same small number of dark rooms, over and over, for tens of thousands of years. Whether they were praying to the bear or just living in its leftovers, they could not stop thinking about it. It's in the art, it's in the bones, it's in the choice of which cave to walk into.

### CHAPTER 5 — The bear didn't lose a war — 6:35–7:55

It's tempting to say we killed them. That's the version with a hero in it. The evidence doesn't support it.
The cave bear disappeared around twenty-four thousand years ago. And the timing matters: that's a period of huge climate and vegetation change, when the open, cold, plant-rich landscapes these animals depended on were shifting, and when their very slow reproduction — one or two cubs, years apart — became a liability instead of a strategy.
[VERIFY: extinction timing (~24,000 BP), dietary/vegetation dependence, and the demographic argument — cite the ancient-DNA and isotope work]

Humans were part of the pressure. Hunting, and taking the caves, and being very good at both. But the honest sentence is: we were one of the reasons, not the reason.
And that's worth sitting with, because it's the opposite of how these stories usually get told.

### CHAPTER 6 — What was actually in the room — 7:55–8:40

So: a bear bigger than any alive today. A vegetarian that would not back down. A house that both species needed. And, at the end of it, a skull on a rock in the dark.
The first room our ancestors ever decorated was somebody else's bedroom.
They walked into a bear's house, in the winter, in the dark, carrying fire — and then they made a place in it for the animal they had displaced.
That's not a hunting story. That's a moving-in story.

### CLOSE — 8:40–8:55

There are still bears in caves. Fewer, and smaller, and in fewer places.
And there are still people who walk into the dark with a light, and stop, and look at something on a rock, and feel that this place was somebody's before it was ours.
The skull is still there. It's just not a bear's any more.
