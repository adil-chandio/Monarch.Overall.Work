## 2. Narration script — FINAL, fact-checked

> Delivery: low, calm, close. **1,567 spoken words = 8:48 @ 178 wpm + 31s scripted pauses = 9:19 (559s).**

### COLD OPEN — 0:00–0:48

Somewhere between thirty-seven thousand and twenty-eight thousand years ago, someone walked into a cave in the dark.
Past the point where daylight gives up.
And they put a bear's skull on a rock.

We can date the room. We cannot date the gesture.

Not dropped. Not thrown. **Placed.**

We don't know their name. But we know the animal it belonged to: the largest bear our species ever lived beside.

And here's the number that ruins the thumbnail. **Six hundred kilos** — which is roughly the biggest brown bear alive today. Not bigger.

`[PAUSE]` So why is *this* the one our ancestors were afraid of?

Three things by the end. How big it really was. Why you could not avoid it. And what a skull on a rock, tens of thousands of years later, is still trying to say.

---

### CHAPTER 1 — 0:48–1:33 · "The shape, not the size"

Take the number seriously, because the number is not the story.

What made this animal different was the shape. A skull with a forehead so steep it looks like a different design. Bones that are heavy even for a bear. A jaw built for grinding, not slicing. Take the biggest brown bear you've ever seen and rebuild it around those three things. That's the cave bear. `[PAUSE]`

And here's the part nobody mentions. It could stand in the entrance of a cave and **fill it** — not because it was a monster, but because you were standing in the only door, and the door happened to be bear-shaped.

`[LOOP]` So what was a six-hundred-kilo animal eating to get that big? Because it was not hunting you.

---

### CHAPTER 2 — 1:33–2:01 · "It ate plants"

The teeth say it. The chemistry of the bones says it. In most places, in most populations, the cave bear was a plant eater. Not a hunter. Not a killer of men.

A huge, slow, herbivorous animal that lived in caves.

`[PAUSE]` One population in Romania was eating meat. `[VERIFY]` So it was not a rule. It was a habit.

Which should make it harmless.

`[SILENCE — 2 seconds]`

It does the opposite.

---

### CHAPTER 3 — 2:01–2:48 · "Why that's worse"

A predator **has a calculation.** A wolf that wants to eat you weighs the risk, and usually decides you are not worth the injury. That is why most predators leave people alone. We are not worth the damage.

A plant-eating bear in its own bedroom makes no calculation at all. It has no reason to leave. It has nothing to be impressed by. You are not food. You are **furniture that's in the way.**

`[PAYOFF — Loop B]` **The animal that isn't afraid of you is the one you can't bluff.**

That's the whole episode, honestly. Everything after this is what happened because of it.

`[LOOP A]` Because here's the thing. This animal lived in caves. So did we. For a very long time. Same country. Same rock. Sometimes the same address.

And it was afraid of nothing in that room.

---

### CHAPTER 4 — 2:48–3:42 · "The same address"

Start with the bears, because the bears were there first.

Thousands of bear skeletons have come out of single cave systems. Not because bears were killed there — because they went there to sleep, and some never woke up. `[VERIFY]`

Now put a human family on that landscape. It's winter. You need shelter, and in limestone country shelter means one thing.

`[PAUSE]` The same cave. The same warm, dry, dark cave that something else is currently asleep in.

At Chauvet, where we started, humans and bears both used it for thousands of years — but **not at the same time.** One, then the other. The room changed hands.

At other sites it's the opposite. In Italy, in two caves called Rio Secco and Fumane, Neanderthals left more than **seventeen hundred** bear bones, with cut marks and stone tools among them. `[VERIFY]` Same cave. Same layers. And they were not waiting for the bear to leave.

---

### CHAPTER 5 — 3:42–4:21 · "The two doors"

So imagine you're the one who decides.

Option one: find another cave. There might not be one inside a day's walk — and a day's walk in winter, with children, is its own sentence.

Option two: take this one.

Understand what that means. It's not a hunt. You can't surround anything in a tunnel. You can't see. Your footsteps come back off the walls and tell the animal exactly where you are. One exit, and you're standing in it, holding a fire that has to stay lit.

`[SILENCE — 2 seconds]`

`[WHISPER]` Whatever they did next, they did it in the dark, with very little room for error.

---

### CHAPTER 6 — 4:21–5:32 · "What the bones admit"

This is where most videos start inventing. So here's what we actually have.

No painting of a man spearing a bear. Something more specific. In a cave in Germany called Hohle Fels, there is a cave bear vertebra with a **stone point still stuck in it.** `[VERIFY]` Not near it. In it. Somebody hit that animal hard enough to leave the weapon behind, and never got it back out.

Other bones carry cut marks — thin, straight slices, the kind a stone tool leaves. Some are broken open the way you break a bone when you want what's inside. `[VERIFY]`

So humans were in those caves, and **humans were eating bears.** Not in a legend. In the trash.

This wasn't a monster movie. It was a **long, ugly, ordinary dispute over property**, fought across hundreds of generations by people probably more afraid of winter than of the animal.

`[PAUSE]` And here's what tells you how human it was. The Italian caves suggest a strategy, and it's brutally practical: **spring.** `[VERIFY]` Right after hibernation, when the bear is thin and slow and the females have just given birth. Not brave. Not a duel. A calculation, made by people who knew which month the odds were on their side.

---

### CHAPTER 7 — 5:32–5:59 · "The other direction"

`[LOOP C]` Now the other direction, and I'll keep it short, because it's the one that stays with you.

There are human bones in those caves, in bear country, with **carnivore tooth marks** on them. `[VERIFY]` Rare. Genuinely rare — and rare is exactly what you'd expect if these encounters usually went one way.

But rare isn't never.

`[PAUSE]` Once, someone went into a dark room to get their home back, and didn't come out.

---

### CHAPTER 8 — 5:59–7:05 · "The skulls" · `[PAYOFF — Loop A]`

Back to the skull on the rock.

It isn't one skull. In more than one cave, researchers found cave bear skulls that look like they were **put** somewhere — set on ledges, placed in niches, sometimes with long bones crossed underneath. `[VERIFY]`

Out of that came one of the most exciting ideas in archaeology: a bear cult. Our first religion, and it was about an animal.

`[PAUSE]` Then the sceptics arrived. And they were right about a lot. A cave is a terrible place to keep a story. Bones roll. Water moves them. Ice pushes them. Later people move them again. A skull on a ledge might just be a skull that fell.

`[PAUSE]` So take away everything that could be an accident. What's left?

Something narrower, and stranger. Humans and bears walked into **the same small number of dark rooms**, over and over, for thousands of years. And when humans finally painted on walls, one of the first animals they painted — in red, in the dark — was the bear. `[VERIFY]`

Praying to it, or living in its leftovers — they could not stop thinking about it.

---

### CHAPTER 9 — 7:05–7:20 · `[FALSE ENDING]`

`[SLOW]` And that's the story. A bear we couldn't hunt, a house we had to take, and a skull we left on a rock.

`[PAUSE]` Except we haven't said the thing that makes it worse.

---

### CHAPTER 10 — 7:20–8:27 · "The bear didn't lose"

The cave bear disappeared around twenty-four thousand years ago. `[VERIFY]`

That was the worst possible century to be a huge plant-eater in Europe. Being enormous, slow, and having one cub every few years stopped being a strategy and became a liability.

`[PAUSE]` And we were part of the pressure. Hunting. Taking the caves. Being very good at both.

But "we killed it" is not something the evidence lets anyone say. There are two camps, and both are serious. One points at the ice: the cold came in waves, the open plant-rich ground shrank, and an animal that hibernates for months on stored fat is the wrong shape for a shrinking larder. They have the dates.

The other points at us. Their DNA work says the population started falling about **fifty thousand years ago** — long before the worst of the cold. `[VERIFY]` And fifty thousand years ago, the one thing changing in Europe was who was living in it.

Two camps. Both have DNA and dates. Neither has the answer.

What we can say is smaller and uglier. **We made a hard life harder.** Not the blade. The last few degrees.

---

### CHAPTER 11 — 8:27–9:19 · "The room"

So back to the skull, one last time.

Think about what that person did. They walked into the dark, into a place that belonged to something bigger than them, that could fill the only door. And then they made a place for the animal they'd displaced. Not a trophy. A **position.** On a rock. In the dark.

`[PAUSE]` The first room our ancestors ever decorated was somebody else's bedroom. They moved into a bear's house, and then they put its head on a shelf.

`[SLOW]` There are still bears in caves. Fewer. Smaller. In fewer places. And there are still people who walk into the dark with a light, look at something on a rock, and understand that this place was somebody's before it was ours.

`[PAUSE]` The skull is still there. It's just not a bear's any more.
