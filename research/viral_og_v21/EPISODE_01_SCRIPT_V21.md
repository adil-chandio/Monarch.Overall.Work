## 2. Narration script (English, ~1,610 words, ~9:20 at 172 wpm)

> Delivery: calm, low, close-mic. No "hey guys", no "in this video". Every `[VERIFY]` must be checked before recording — see §6.

### COLD OPEN (0:00–0:38) — hook, no logo, no intro

Imagine you're the last one awake.
The fire has burned down to a red breath. Everyone else is asleep under the same rock you've slept under your whole life. And outside, in the dark, something is dragging a branch across the ground.
You are not afraid of the dark. You are afraid of what happens *after* — because in this world, a body is not a private thing.
When someone dies here, it isn't only their family who has to decide what to do. It's everyone. And they have to decide **today**, because tomorrow the smell will bring things with teeth.
What they did next is one of the strangest, most human things in the entire archaeological record. And it started long before anyone had a word for "grave".

### CHAPTER 1 — "Before there were graves" (0:38–2:05)

For most of human prehistory, there is no grave. There is no marker, no plot, no stone with a name. For hundreds of thousands of years, the dead were simply... left. Or lost.
And that word matters: **lost**. A body left on the surface is not a person any more. It's food, and it's weather, and it's time. Vultures, hyenas, rain, rot. Within weeks there is nothing left that looks like a person.
So when archaeologists find a body that was *deliberately* placed — arranged, curled, covered, given an object — that is a signal. It means somebody looked at a dead human and decided the body should be somewhere specific, in a position that meant something.
That decision is one of the oldest traces we have of a very particular kind of mind: a mind that believes the dead are still, in some way, *someone*.
[VERIFY: earliest accepted deliberate burials — Qafzeh and Skhul, Israel, roughly 100,000+ years ago; simpler "body caching" claims at Rising Star Cave are contested — treat as contested, do not state as fact]

But here's the uncomfortable part. Not every "burial" in the record is a burial. Some bodies ended up in pits because a cave floor was convenient. Some "grave goods" were things that fell. Archaeologists have spent a century arguing about which is which — and in this video, we're going to keep that argument visible instead of pretending it's settled.

### CHAPTER 2 — "The curl" (2:05–3:40)

Look at enough prehistoric burials and a shape repeats: the body curled on its side, knees drawn up, hands near the face.
It looks like sleep. That's why early researchers called it the "sleeping position", and immediately decided it meant something tender — a family laying someone down for the last time.
The problem is that it's also just physics. A body that's carried a short distance and dropped into a shallow pit will often end up curled. And a body that dries or is bound before burial holds whatever shape it was given.
So which is it? We can't know. But we can say something narrower and more interesting: the curl is **reproducible**. Across continents, across tens of thousands of years, people kept putting bodies in the same posture. Whatever the reason, it was a *tradition* — passed from person to person, generation to generation.
And a tradition about the dead is a tradition about the living.
[VERIFY: "flexed/sleeping position" interpretation and its critique — cite a standard osteoarchaeology text before recording]

### CHAPTER 3 — "Red" (3:40–5:05)

Here's the detail that gets people. In a number of very old burials, the bones are stained red.
The pigment is ochre — an iron-rich clay that people were already grinding and using for paint, for skin, for marking things. In some burials it's scattered over the body. In others the body lies on a bed of it. Sometimes a lump of ochre is placed in the hand.
Now, the sceptical reading is easy: ochre is everywhere in these sites, it's used for tanning hides, it gets on everything. A red-stained skeleton might mean nothing more than a messy workshop.
But the pattern is hard to explain away, because the ochre is often **only** on the burial. Not on the floor around it. Not in the other layers. On the person.
[VERIFY: ochre burial examples and the "only on the burial" claim — must be tied to named sites in the final script]

If that's intentional, then someone stood over a dead body and made a choice that had no survival value at all. It didn't keep the body warm. It didn't keep animals away. It cost time and pigment, in a life where both were scarce.
That is the moment prehistory stops being about calories and starts being about meaning.

### CHAPTER 4 — "The flowers" (5:05–6:30)

In 1951, in a cave in northern Iraq called Shanidar, a team found a Neanderthal man in a shallow grave. He was old by the standards of his time, and badly injured — a withered arm, a damaged eye socket, injuries that would have needed years of help from other people to survive.
And in the soil around him, they found pollen.
The story that followed is one of the most famous in all of archaeology: the "flower burial". A community laying a disabled elder to rest with flowers.
Then, decades later, the story cracked. The pollen could have come from burrowing bees. It could have blown in. It could have been carried by rodents. Careful reanalysis turned a poetic certainty into a maybe.
[VERIFY: Shanidar IV "flower burial" — Solecki 1950s; the bee-nest critique (Sommer 1999) and later re-examinations; state the current state of the debate accurately]

And honestly? The maybe is better television than the certainty.
Because the man himself is the evidence. He lived for years with injuries that would have made him slow, painful, and expensive to keep alive. Somebody brought him food. Somebody cleaned him. Somebody didn't leave him behind.
Whatever was in that soil, **that** is the part we know.

### CHAPTER 5 — "The things they left" (6:30–7:55)

Across the record, some graves contain objects: a shell, a tool, a pierced tooth, an animal's horn.
The instinct is to read them as gifts. A pocket watch for the afterlife.
The problem is that an object in a grave can arrive in at least five ways: it was placed, it was worn and never removed, it was dropped by a mourner, it fell in later, or it was already in the ground when the grave was dug. Archaeologists have to eliminate each one, and often can't.
What survives the doubt is simpler and stranger: **some people were buried with more than others, very early**.
[VERIFY: any specific claim about differential grave goods in the Palaeolithic — needs a named site and a named source]

And the moment a grave can hold more than another grave, a body becomes a statement. Not just "this person died" — but "this person mattered in a way that has to be shown."
We tend to think status is a modern invention. The dirt says otherwise.

### CHAPTER 6 — "Why it matters now" (7:55–8:55)

So what do you actually do, standing at the edge of a hole, with someone you loved?
You do something unnecessary. You curl them. You colour them. You put something in their hand. You say words that have no effect on anything.
That's the through-line from a 100,000-year-old pit to a modern funeral with flowers nobody will see again. The behaviour is almost absurd — it costs and returns nothing — and we have never stopped doing it.
Which suggests the thing being buried was never only the body. It was the relationship. The grave is for the person who has to walk back to the fire alone.
[VERIFY: any comparative claim about modern funerals — keep it qualitative, no statistics]

### CLOSE (8:55–9:20)

Tonight, somewhere, someone is sitting up later than everyone else, in a room that's too quiet, thinking about a person who isn't there.
That's not a modern feeling. It's one of the oldest things we have evidence for.
And the oldest thing we do about it is not to fix it — it's to make a place for it. A pit. A curl. A handful of red dust.
If you want to know what people believed before they could write it down, don't look at the sky. Look at the ground, and see what they refused to just leave there.
