No painting of a man spearing a bear. Something more specific. In a cave in Germany called Hohle Fels, there is a cave bear vertebra with a stone point still stuck in it.  Not near it. In it. Somebody hit that animal hard enough to leave the weapon behind, and never got it back out.
Other bones carry cut marks — thin, straight slices, the kind a stone tool leaves. Some are broken open the way you break a bone when you want what's inside. 
So humans were in those caves, and humans were eating bears. Not in a legend. In the trash.
This wasn't a monster movie. It was a long, ugly, ordinary dispute over property, fought across hundreds of generations by people probably more afraid of winter than of the animal.
 And here's what tells you how human it was. The Italian caves suggest a strategy, and it's brutally practical: spring.  Right after hibernation, when the bear is thin and slow and the females have just given birth. Not brave. Not a duel. A calculation, made by people who knew which month the odds were on their side.
